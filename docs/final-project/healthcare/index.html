It is this extracted version of the data that we use in the Midterm Vignette below.

Midterm Vignette

Given the prominence of healthcare providers and health insurance organizations among the firms hiring data scientists (see the top of the main datasets page), the goal for this dataset is to provide an “inside look” at the types of events and information formats that might stream into a healthcare organization’s OLTP database.

For Midterm purposes, you can imagine being hired as a data scientist at a hospital (congratulations!) and being asked to use your DSAN 6000 skills to meet the following needs:

  1. The hospital is getting lots of complaints about Medication Requests not being processed, so it would like to take the Medication Request events streaming into their existing OLTP database and acquire a sense for (e.g.) how many non-processed requests there are per week, and why this might be happening (for example, as in this famous study of judicial rulings before and after lunch breaks, it may be due to doctor fatigue)
  2. On top of your own analyic findings regarding the Medication Requests issue, the hospital would also like to have an Administrative Dashboard wherein the data you used to derive your conclusions (in the previous step) is made continuously available to hospital administrators. Among other benefits, this would ensure that the effects of changes implemented by the hospital to address the issue can be monitored over time.

As part of this task, you have been given access to the hospital’s OLTP database (generated by Synthea, and detailed below), and you have been told to “take it from there”: your job, on Questions 1-6 on the Midterm, is to plan out what each step in an OLTP \(\leadsto\) OLAP \(\leadsto\) analytic insights pipeline for this task might look like.

Note the complexities that may be involved in turning this OLTP-format stream of events into an analytics-friendly OLAP infrastructure meeting the hospital’s needs (as listed above). For example:

  • A simple issue could just be de-duplication: One doctor might think that the person they’re treating is a new patient, for example, but the person’s information might actually have been entered earlier by a different doctor (and, the information might in fact be slightly different! I accidentally tell doctors “Jeff” instead of my technical legal name “Jeffrey”, for example, and it is often entered by doctors as “Jeffery”, “Geoff”, “Geoffrey”, etc. 😵‍💫)
  • A more complex issue that we’ve discussed in earlier weeks could be data provenance (also referred to as “data lineage” sometimes): perhaps the failing medication requests are being entered by a doctor’s assistant, and the system thus detects that the assistant is not authorized to make medication requests, so that they are subsequently denied.
  • How might you handle a case where someone’s custodian or legal guardian is the one actually present at the appointment, such that the medication request was made during an encounter with person \(X\) (the guardian) but the medication itself is for a patient \(Y\)?
